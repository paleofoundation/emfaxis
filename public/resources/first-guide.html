1. Write the question first

Before opening a paper, write the decision you are trying to understand in one sentence. “Are EMFs bad?” is too broad to guide this exercise. “Does research on mobile-phone call time answer my question about a wireless device in a bedroom?” gives you something concrete to compare with a study. Add the population and outcome that matter to the question, then leave a separate space for what the paper actually studied. The point is to expose any mismatch before an alarming or reassuring headline becomes your conclusion.

Treat your first sentence as a question, not a claim you must defend. A useful reading session can end with “this study does not answer it.” Save the citation and the reason it is relevant or indirect; do not force a yes or no answer from every source. You can use the worksheet at the end without recording a name, address, diagnosis or other personal detail. It is designed for comparing a public claim with a research record, not for building a health profile.

2. Keep the exposure attached

Give the exposure its own line in your notes. Copy the source or device, the frequency if reported, the exposure measure, the duration and the comparison condition. Write “not stated in the material I read” when a field is missing. Do not quietly replace a paper’s measure with one you would prefer it had used. Also record whether you read an abstract, a registry entry or the complete paper. That small label prevents the much larger mistake of treating unavailable methods as if they had been checked.

The examples in this guide do not all measure the same thing. COSMOS used mobile-phone call-time information, while INTERPHONE relied on recalled phone use.[1][2] The sleep experiment tested specified signals before sleep, and the corrected isolated-sperm paper described an experimental RF dose range.[3][4] Keeping those descriptions visible is a practical safeguard against moving a conclusion between unlike setups. An honest comparison may need to stop at “different exposure; relevance unresolved.”

3. Read the comparison before the conclusion

In your notes, describe how the groups or conditions were compared. Was the question about people followed over time, people recalling earlier exposure, an experimental exposure against a sham condition, or cells in a laboratory? Locate that information in the methods if you have access to the full text. If you only have the abstract, keep your description at the level it supports. Avoid filling gaps from what studies of that type usually do; this exercise is about the particular record in front of you.

COSMOS is recorded here as a human cohort, INTERPHONE as a case-control study, and the sleep experiment as a randomized trial.[1][2][3] The NTP rat work and the isolated-sperm experiment use different biological systems from those human studies.[5][4] In a comparison table, keep those design labels beside the findings instead of giving every paper a single vote. The aim is to understand the contribution of each source, not to announce a winner by counting titles.

4. Name the outcome precisely

Write the endpoint using the paper’s own level of specificity. Keep a separate box for the broader claim made by the headline. If the two boxes differ, ask what evidence connects them. Do not supply that bridge yourself. For numerical results, copy the estimate, its units, comparison and uncertainty interval together, then check the source location. If any of those pieces is missing, flag the number for verification before repeating it. This draft deliberately avoids reproducing effect estimates that would require a fresh numerical audit.

The selected sleep record describes a genotype-dependent change in sleep-spindle frequency, rather than a demonstration that everyday exposure causes insomnia.[3] The sperm-cell experiment reports laboratory endpoints and does not establish infertility from ordinary phone carrying.[4] The preconception-cohort record concerns time to pregnancy and reports little overall association with front-pocket carrying, alongside a subgroup finding.[6] Reading these together is an exercise in naming different outcomes, not a combined verdict about reproductive or sleep health.

5. Four worked examples

Use the examples below as models for a bounded summary. Each gives a question, the source information available in the current catalogue, an interpretation boundary and a next reading task. These are not full appraisals. All remain based on previously checked abstracts or abstract-level catalogue records, with scientific appraisal and independent human review pending. The suggested next tasks are deliberately questions for source review; they are not claims that the work has already been completed.

A. “Did a phone study settle cancer risk?”

What the records say: COSMOS did not find an association between cumulative call time and the brain tumors examined.[1] INTERPHONE’s overall results did not show increased risk, but its highest recalled call-time category showed elevated glioma odds.[2] The INTERPHONE record also identifies recall and participation bias as barriers to a causal interpretation.[2]

Keep the boundary: A bounded summary should retain both the study conditions and the conflicting pattern, without turning either record into a universal promise or a personal risk estimate. Next reading task: Inspect the exposure assessment, follow-up, missing data and bias discussion in both full texts. Record how those features affect the exact question you wrote down. Do not label that appraisal complete until it is actually documented.

B. “Does a brain-wave change mean harm?”

What the record says: The selected sleep experiment reported a sleep-spindle change in one genotype group after one tested signal.[3] The catalogue identifies this as a physiological finding whose clinical meaning and generalizability remain uncertain.[3]

Keep the boundary: Preserve the difference between the measured feature and the everyday complaint that brought you to the article. Next reading task: Locate the prespecified outcomes, analysis groups, sham comparison and any reported measures of experienced sleep. Mark each as found, not reported in the source you read, or not yet checked. An unanswered methods question should remain visible instead of being converted into either reassurance or alarm.

C. “Can an animal finding give my risk?”

What the record says: The NTP rat report judged the evidence for malignant heart schwannomas in exposed male rats to be clear, with judgments differing across sexes and tumors.[5] Its whole-body dose, exposure schedule, species and survival differences prevent a direct conversion into a risk estimate for human phone use.[5]

Keep the boundary: Neither dismiss the result nor turn it into a calculator for an individual. Next reading task: Write down the specific biological system, exposure, endpoint and comparison. Then list which human question you hope to answer and what additional evidence would be needed to connect the two. Leave that connection unresolved if you cannot trace it to an appropriate source and an explicit appraisal.

D. “Is one measurement a health verdict?”

What the record says: The Ahlbom pooled analysis concerns estimated residential magnetic-field exposure and childhood leukemia; its higher exposure category was associated with increased risk, with causation unresolved.[7] The catalogue explicitly states that the category boundary is not a proven safety threshold.[7]

Keep the boundary: Put a household observation and a study’s exposure definition in separate worksheet boxes before comparing them. Next reading task: Document what instrument produced the observation, the units, range, location, operating conditions and averaging period. If you cannot match the research definition, write “comparison not established.” This worksheet does not interpret measurements, set pass/fail limits or replace an appropriately qualified assessment.

6. Make uncertainty useful

Use three different labels in your notes: “not available to me,” “not yet checked” and “not reported in the source I read.” They point to different next steps. The first may require access to a paper or supplement; the second requires reading or verification; the third describes the source you inspected without making a claim about all possible evidence. None should become a blank cell that later looks like a zero, a negative result or a finding of safety.

The HERMES catalogue record is a useful example of why an analysis label belongs beside a conclusion. Its full-sample uncertainty interval included no difference, while selected analyses suggested an association with memory change; modeled exposure and confounding limit causal inference.[8] A reading note that only says “memory declined” would lose that distinction. For the next appraisal, ask which analysis supports the proposed sentence, whether the result was specified in advance, and how confidently it answers the reader’s question. The current draft does not claim to have completed those checks.

Check a notice against the identity of the paper it actually updates. The De Iuliis paper has a 2013 correction that changes the biological replicate count to three and corrects the values in Figure 1D.[9] Keep that notice attached to the original source. This edition uses the corrected record only to illustrate experimental context and interpretation limits; its full scientific appraisal remains pending. A correction notice is not an additional independent experiment.

When two papers appear to disagree, make a short difference list before choosing a side. Compare the population, exposure measure, endpoint, timing and design. Also ask whether reports reuse the same study participants; do not assume every new citation means a new independent experiment. Keep corrections, retractions and supplementary files on the review checklist. The De Iuliis correction has been checked for this revision; a complete correction and retraction audit of the remaining bibliography stays open for the release reviewer.

7. Turn a headline into a source trail

Open the companion worksheet or print it for a reading session. Start with one claim and one source. Fill in the exposure, population, outcome and comparison using only the material you actually inspected. Add a source locator precise enough that another reader can find the same passage. For an abstract, record the abstract section; for a full text, record the page, section, table or figure. Leave unsupported fields explicitly unfilled rather than guessing them.

Finish with two sentences: “This source addresses…” and “It does not by itself establish…”. Then choose the next action from finding the full text, checking a number, comparing another source, requesting a qualified interpretation, or stopping because the source does not answer your question. You do not need to reach a personal health decision to complete the exercise. A clear boundary and a working source link are useful outcomes in their own right.

The main lesson

Keep the claim attached to the exact exposure, population, outcome, comparison and source limits. If a paper does not answer your question, record the gap.

  1. Question: What am I trying to understand?
  2. Source: What did I actually read, and where is the passage?
  3. Exposure: What was tested or estimated, and for how long?
  4. People or system: Who or what was studied?
  5. Comparison: What was the reference group or condition?
  6. Outcome: What changed, and what was not measured?
  7. Limits: What keeps this from answering a broader claim?
  8. Next step: Which specific gap should be checked next?

For this edition, the worked examples remain abstract-level reading exercises. Do not describe them as a comprehensive evidence review or completed risk assessment.
